\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{The loss as the variable nobody examined}
The method for refuting the claims in \cite{shwartzziv2017} builds on
controlled comparison, changing pieces as covered in
\autoref{sec:compression} to show how components of the experimental setup had
been overlooked as potential contributors to the observed compression. The
choice of loss function is left as a stone unturned however. The only time
Mean Squared Error (MSE) appears is in the linear network, in which the
nonlinearity was changed as well. The loss is therefore \textit{not} shown to
be irrelevant to the compression, it is varied without regard to its effect.
\autoref{tab:permutations} lists the permutations -- i.e. loss/activation
functions -- which are run jointly across the two papers.

\subsection{The growing weights}
Put simply, the argument in \cite{saxe2018} is that compression occurs as a
result of a two step process; first the weights grow and consequently the
activations get saturated, and since tanh is double-saturating they land close
to either $-1$ or $1$, which results in a low amount of measured information.
The latter step is well covered, whereas the first is simply assumed. They
don't ask why the weights grow to begin with.

One candidate explanation to the growing weights could be the cross-entropy
function, which is defined as
\begin{equation}
	CE = -\sum_{i=1}^{N} q_i\log_2(p_i)
\end{equation}
where $q$ is the target and $p$ the predicted distribution over the $N$
classes. For a one-hot target this reduces to $-\log_2 p_c$ on the correct
class $c$, which only reaches zero as $p_c\to1$. The cross-entropy loss
therefore has no finite minimum once the training data is separated, since a
probability of $1$ is only reached in the limit of infinitely large weights,
and thus optimization keeps growing the weights long after the fitting is
done. MSE has a minimum at finite weights, and the gradient-based optimization
thus stops once the outputs sit on such a minimum.

In \cite{soudry2018} they study this directly, and show that for losses with
an exponential tail, such as the cross-entropy, gradient descent grows the
weights as $\log t$ whilst their direction settles on the max-margin solution.
Their motivation is stated as explaining the benefit of continuing to optimize
the cross-entropy after the training error is already zero, which is the same
stretch of training that \cite{shwartzziv2017} report as the compression
phase. It should be said that this is proven for linear predictors on
separable data, and only extended to deep networks in a restricted form, so
for the networks discussed in this paper, it isn't directly applicable, but it
motivates the exploration of the role played by the loss function.

\subsection{What this explains}
This does not replace the explanation given in \cite{saxe2018}, but rather
adds a cause to the argument. The double-saturating nonlinearity is the
filter which turns growing weights into a reduction in the measured $I(X;T)$,
whilst the loss is what makes them grow in the first place. Both are needed
and neither is sufficient on its own.

The networks trained with full batch gradient descent compress just as much as
the ones trained with SGD, which they report as a fact against
\cite{shwartzziv2017}. Under this explanation it is what one would expect,
since nothing in the mechanism needs randomness, and the result in
\cite{soudry2018} is for plain deterministic gradient descent. The ReLU
networks still do not compress, also as expected, as growing weights only
spread the activations over more bins. The linear network settles nothing
either way, as it has neither a saturating nonlinearity nor a loss which
drives the weights.

The growth itself is not assumed. In the appendices of \cite{saxe2018} the
norm of the weights in each layer is reported to increase during training, for
the tanh as well as the ReLU networks. All of them are trained with the cross-
entropy however, so this just tells us that the weights grow and not what
makes them grow.

\subsection{The missing permutation}
The same networks trained with MSE, everything else held fixed, would separate
the two explanations. If the compression weakens or disappears then the loss
is doing part of the work, and compression is not a function of the
nonlinearity alone. If it compresses in a similar fashion then the
nonlinearity argument suffices on its own and the conjecture is dead, which
would only serve to strengthen the arguments in \cite{saxe2018}. Either
outcome would settle what the two papers leave open, which is whether the
nonlinearity alone explains the compression, or whether the loss which grows
the weights is part of the explanation.
